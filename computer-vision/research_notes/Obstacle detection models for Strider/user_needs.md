# User needs: what blind and low-vision pedestrians need from a camera obstacle aid (Strider)

Research date: 2026-11-14. Older findings are marked with their year. Several primary sources (Manduchi & Kurniawan full text, PMC Hersh 2022 review, Nature Comms 2025, medRxiv 2025 ETA study, RNIB 2025 PDF) could not be fetched in full (TLS error, 403, bot wall, or binary PDF), so some figures come from search snippets or secondary citations. These are flagged.

## Q1. Which hazards cause the most collisions, injuries, or falls, and which does the cane or dog miss?

### Takeaway
The best evidence points to two gaps the cane does not cover: **head- and chest-height obstacles** (branches, signs, gates, bars, truck mirrors) and **ground-level risks the user can't anticipate** (drop-offs, stairs, open manholes, uneven or cracked pavement). In UK street surveys the most common obstacles are **cars parked on pavements, temporary clutter (bins, A-boards) and sharing space with cyclists and e-scooters**. A COCO class list limited to person, vehicle, bike, dog and bag misses most of the head-level and drop-off hazards.

### Cited Findings
- **Manduchi & Kurniawan (2010 tech report, 2011 AER Journal):** survey interview of more than 300 blind or legally blind people about the frequency, nature and causes of head-level accidents (bumping into things at chest height or above) and falls while walking. Report UCSC-SOE-10-24, dated 2010-07-29. — [UCSC tech report page](https://tr.soe.ucsc.edu/research/technical-reports/UCSC-SOE-10-24); [PDF](https://tr.soe.ucsc.edu/sites/default/files/technical-reports/UCSC-SOE-10-24.pdf)
- **Secondary readings of Manduchi & Kurniawan conflict:** one paper says over 40% of respondents had head-level accidents at least once a month. Hersh (2022) reports about 40% at least once a year and about 15% once a month. Another source says more than 50% had one at least once a year. A further paper cites an 88% lifetime figure. The Hersh reading looks the most careful, but the primary text was not verified. — [Search summary incl. Hersh 2022 / Glasgow eprint](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/271318/1/271318.pdf); [PMC5366249 citing paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5366249)
- **GuideTouch (arXiv 2026):** cites Blanco et al. (2010) that "more than 40% of blind individuals report head-level injuries or falls every few months, regardless of mobility aids". In the team's interviews with 28 visually impaired people (aged 16 to 60), respondents named three kinds of obstacle the cane misses:
  - head-level hazards (road signs, gates, horizontal bars)
  - drop-offs and ground-level risks (open manholes, stairs)
  - thin objects (poles, bollards, fencing)

  Replacing the white cane was described as "highly undesirable". — [GuideTouch arXiv 2601.13813](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.13813v2)
- **Cane limits:** the cane is effective for ground-level obstacles close to the user (under about 1 m). It does not detect obstacles that are not standing on the ground (e.g., an open window) or obstacles farther away. — [GuideTouch arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.13813v2); [Hersh 2022 (Glasgow eprint)](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/271318/1/271318.pdf)
- **RNIB poll, 2025, ~1,200 UK respondents** (reported by LocalGov, Sept 2025):
  - **Main barriers:**
    - 82% cite vehicles parked on pavements
    - 71% cite cracked or uneven pavements
    - 56% cite temporary obstacles such as bin bags and A-boards
    - 52% cite sharing space with cyclists or e-scooters
    - 44% cite a shortage of accessible crossings
  - **Effect on travel:** about 61% can't make all the walking journeys they need, and only 9% feel safe walking independently in their neighbourhood.
  - **Injuries:** 11% of injured respondents needed hospital treatment.
  
  — [LocalGov 2025](https://www.localgov.co.uk/Street-hazards-endangering-blind-pedestrians-charity-warns/62969)
- **RNIB "In My Way" (Feb 2025 survey, 1,197 respondents):**
  - 92% said they have to walk into the road to avoid cars, bikes and e-scooters obstructing pavements.
  - Respondents reported broken bones, chronic pain, lasting mobility impairment and psychological injury from collisions.
  - Near-misses reduce willingness to go out.
  
  These figures come from a search summary; the PDF body text could not be extracted. — [RNIB In My Way 2025 PDF](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/In_My_Way_-_Navigating_pedestrian_journeys_with_sight_loss_2025_PDF.pdf); [RNIB e-scooter news](https://rnib.org.uk/news/e-scooter-challenge-safety-on-streets)
- **Guide Dogs UK:**
  - 77% of blind or partially sighted respondents say e-scooter rider behaviour makes them feel unsafe.
  - The charity calls for a legally required universal sound on e-scooters.
  - DfT figures: 52 pedestrians were seriously injured in collisions with e-scooters in England in 2023.
  
  These are secondary, from news coverage. — [Guide Dogs transport campaign](https://www.guidedogs.org.uk/how-you-can-help/campaigning/our-current-campaigns/transport/); [Optician Online](https://www.opticianonline.net/content/news/e-scooters-raise-collision-concerns-for-blind-people); [Irish News, 2024 e-scooter casualties](https://www.irishnews.com/news/uk/e-scooter-crashes-leave-one-pedestrian-dead-and-59-seriously-injured-in-2024-QG4NQEK4SNO2ZBLCRUW3KSFLAM/)
- **Quiet vehicles:**
  - **Pliskow et al. (SAE, 2011):** blind pedestrians detected a hybrid with added sound at a greater distance. Three sounds performed significantly better than the same hybrid with no added sound.
  - **UNECE evaluation (24 legally blind participants):** detection distances at 10 km/h were not significantly different from traditional EVs.
  - **Penn State test-track work:** found cases where the probability of detecting a quiet vehicle never reached 100%, even at close range.
  - **Louisiana Tech (2015):** results are reported contradictorily across sources.
  - **Salford e-scooter AVAS study (88 participants, 35 blind or partially sighted):** a +2 dBA sound reduced detection time by 0.48 s.
  
  — [SAE 2011-01-1725](https://saemobilus.sae.org/papers/detection-hybrid-quiet-vehicles-blind-visually-impaired-pedestrians-2011-01-1725); [Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/blind-pedestrians-and-quieter-vehicles-how-adding-artificial-soun-2/); [Salford repository](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/3644109); [News-Medical 2015](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20150603/Researchers-conduct-noise-output-measurements-on-electric-cars-to-improve-safety-of-blind-and-visually-impaired-people.aspx)
- **WeWALK smart cane:** cannot detect ground-level drops such as stairs, curbs and holes. Its ultrasonic sensor targets obstacles between waist and head level and aims to detect them at least 1 m ahead. — [WeWALK support](https://support.wewalk.io/en/article/are-there-any-obstacles-objects-that-wewalk-cannot-detect-3/); [AppleVis WeWALK 2](https://www.applevis.com/comment/188781)

### Inferences
- **Proposed priority ranking for Strider**, weighting injury severity, frequency and the gap left by cane and dog:
  1. **Head- and chest-height protrusions:** branches, signs, scaffolding bars, truck mirrors, open car or tailgate doors.
  2. **Moving vehicles, cyclists and e-scooters on or near the path,** especially quiet ones. These are high severity and hard to hear.
  3. **Drop-offs:** curbs without ramps, stairs down, holes, open manholes. These cause falls. A long cane often finds them, but late. A chest camera aimed forward and down can only partly see them.
  4. **Pavement-parked cars and parked e-scooters or bikes** that block the path. These are frequent, and the cane does find them, so the value is in early path-replanning rather than collision avoidance.
  5. **Thin vertical obstacles** (poles, bollards) and **temporary clutter** (bins, A-boards, construction barriers).
  6. **Uneven or cracked surfaces.** Very common, but hard for a VGA chest camera.
- **What the current COCO subset covers:** person, car, bike, dog and bag roughly cover item 2 and part of item 4.
  - COCO has "stop sign", "fire hydrant", "bench" and "parking meter", but not poles, bollards, branches, barriers, mirrors, curbs, stairs or holes.
  - Items 1, 3 and 5 therefore need either open-vocabulary detection, segmentation, depth, or custom-trained classes.
- **Guide dog users:** a trained dog generally steers around obstacles, including some overhead ones. Strider's value is likely greatest for cane users. This is my inference; no source was retrieved for it.

### Gaps
- The Manduchi & Kurniawan full text could not be retrieved (TLS error, then a fetch permission denial). The exact percentages, the causes breakdown (e.g., tree branches vs. signs) and the cane-vs-dog differences are unverified.
- No quantitative breakdown was found of which specific objects (truck mirrors, open car doors, construction barriers) cause the most collisions.
- No US-specific (NFB or ACB) survey data on street clutter or e-scooters was retrieved.

## Q2. What do blind users say they want, and what do they dislike?

### Takeaway
Users want a **hands-free supplement that covers what the cane misses (head level, drop-offs, thin objects)** and does not replace the cane. They dislike **unnecessary or too-frequent alerts** (these add cognitive cost without preventing collisions), alerts that come **too late or never**, bulky or odd-looking hardware, high cost, and "all-in-one" marketing claims.

### Cited Findings
- **Wayfinding efficacy study (medRxiv, 2025), 13 blind or low-vision participants** testing a camera-based vest and an ultrasonic wearable:
  - Participants reported that some obstacle alerts came too late or never fired.
  - The authors found that unnecessary alerts added cognitive cost and slowed users without helping them avoid collisions.
  - Workload was measured with NASA-TLX.
  
  The full text returned 403; findings are from search snippets. — [medRxiv 2025.07.07.25330998](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.07.07.25330998v1.full); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/400170842_Efficacy_of_electronic_travel_aids_for_the_blind_and_visually_impaired_during_wayfinding)
- **BuzzClip vs NOA (medRxiv, 2025):** argues that frequent alerts cause mental fatigue and that more selective, camera-based detection may reduce load. Weak evidence: one sighted, blindfolded participant. — [medRxiv 2025.02.20.25322389](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.02.20.25322389.full.pdf)
- **Wearable travel-aid review (Hersh, 2022):** users should not get over-frequent, unnecessary alerts, which can distract them and cause them to miss real obstacles. Alerts should be limited to obstacles the user could actually collide with. — [PMC9324285](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9324285/) (bot wall; from search summary)
- **2023 systematic review of wearable obstacle-avoidance ETAs:** comfort and cognitive load were reported in 23 studies, but many studies gathered little feedback from intended end users. — [ResearchGate systematic review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/371517045_Wearable_obstacle_avoidance_electronic_travel_aids_for_blind_and_visually_impaired_individuals_A_systematic_review)
- **GuideTouch interviews (n=28):** strong interest in a hands-free wearable for head-level obstacles. Replacing the cane was "highly undesirable". — [arXiv 2601.13813](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.13813v2)
- **AppleVis community on Biped (2024):**
  - Users doubt that current technology can remove the need for a cane ("no way is current tech so accurate that a cane or something touching the ground wouldn't still be required").
  - One said they are "grabbing my cane anyway".
  - The harness form factor was criticised.
  - Another said the company's "all in one" claim "isn't really the case".
  
  — [AppleVis Biped thread](https://applevis.com/comment/172472)
- **Positioning by assistive-tech makers and O&M organisations:**
  - OKO says it is not a replacement for O&M training, a guide dog or a white cane.
  - Apple says Magnifier Detection Mode should not be relied on for navigation or in high-risk situations.
  
  — [OKO / Ophthalmic Edge](https://ophthalmicedge.org/patient/oko-app-makes-walk-signals-accessible); [Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/detect-doors-around-you-iph35c335575/ios)
- **.lumen review caveat:** a five-figure price for unproven technology may not make sense for people who already have effective navigation tools. — [The Gadgeteer, Dec 2025](https://the-gadgeteer.com/2025/12/26/what-happens-when-blind-navigation-stops-being-reactive/)

### Inferences
- False alarms and redundant alerts are the main thing that makes these aids get abandoned. Precision on "will I hit this?" matters more than recall over every object class.
- Strider should **not** announce every detected person or car. It should cue only objects in the walking corridor that are approaching or close, and especially the categories the cane can't reach.
- Late alerts are as damaging as false ones. Users then stop trusting the device.

### Gaps
- No NFB or ACB position statement on camera obstacle-detection wearables was retrieved.
- No quantitative "acceptable false-alarm rate" from user studies was found.

## Q3. Practical requirements: range, warning time, latency, false-alert rate, direction and distance, tracking

### Takeaway
There is no published consensus on preferred warning time. The concrete numbers found are:
- an end-to-end latency target of **at most 320 ms**, based on human reaction time (Nature Comms 2025)
- detection of head-level obstacles from **at least 1 m** (WeWALK)
- ultrasonic wearables with **2 to 4.5 m** ranges (Sunu)
- head and knee obstacles at about 0.5 m, sampled every 100 ms on an ESP32 (GuideTouch)

Users and studies favour **directional, distance-coded cues** (side-specific vibration, rate increasing with proximity).

### Cited Findings
- **Nature Communications (2025), 12 blind and low-vision volunteers:** a wearable obstacle-avoidance device with cross-modal learning set its end-to-end delay target at no more than 320 ms, based on human reaction time. It reported walking trajectory, velocity, collision-avoidance rate, end-to-end delay and power. — [Nature Comms s41467-025-58085-x](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-58085-x) (paywall/redirect; from search snippet)
- **GuideTouch:**
  - **Sensing:** two VL53L5CX ToF sensors (8×8 zones) give a 90° vertical field of view. For a 170 cm user, they detect knee-level (30 cm) and head-level (160 cm) obstacles from 50 cm away, and objects about 4 cm across at 1 m.
  - **Processing:** an ESP32 processes the data every 0.1 s.
  - **Feedback:** four vibration motors on the shoulders and upper chest fire on the side of the obstacle.
  - **Results:** 14 blind users recognised the main directional cues with about 93.7% accuracy. Sighted users recognised simpler 10-pattern sets better (92.9%) than 15-pattern sets (78.4%).
  
  — [arXiv 2601.13813](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.13813v2)
- **WeWALK:** targets head-to-waist obstacles at at least 1 m. It has Off, Short, Mid and Far range settings, and Far is recommended outdoors. — [WeWALK support](https://support.wewalk.io/en/article/customizing-obstacle-detection-settings-through-the-smart-cane-2)
- **Sunu Band:** ultrasonic wrist device. Range is quoted as about 4.5 m outdoors and 2 m indoors, or about 4 m, or 6 ft and 16 ft modes. Sources vary. — [New Atlas](https://newatlas.com/sunu-band-blind/50770); [Lighthouse SF](https://lighthouse-sf.org/navigating-campus-with-the-sunuband-from-adaptations/)
- **Distance-coded haptics:**
  - Smart-glasses example: 200 Hz vibration within 3 m, 150 Hz beyond.
  - A patent for a neck-worn camera maps vibration intensity to distance and makes the mapping user-customisable.
  
  — [Electropages 2025](https://www.electropages.com/blog/2025/05/new-glasses-allows-visually-impaired-people-avoid-obstacles); [US patent 9370459](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9370459)
- **Proximity cues:** Apple Magnifier detection gives sound, speech or haptic feedback that gets more frequent as the user gets closer, and People Detection reports distance. — [Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/detect-doors-around-you-iph35c335575/ios)
- **Moving-object prediction:** Biped NOA says its collar uses wide-FOV 3D cameras (170°) and predicts obstacle trajectories a few seconds ahead. This is a company claim. — [Ophthalmology Times](https://www.ophthalmologytimes.com/view/smart-harness-alerts-visually-impaired-people-to-obstacles-while-walking); [AppleVis](https://applevis.com/comment/171390)

### Inferences (derived; not from a single source)
- **Timing at walking speed (1.2 to 1.4 m/s):**
  - 320 ms of latency is about 0.4 to 0.45 m of travel.
  - The cane's ~1 m reach is only about 0.7 to 0.8 s of warning.
  - To give 2 to 3 s of warning for **static** obstacles (enough to hear a cue, decide and sidestep), Strider needs reliable detection at about **3 to 4 m**.
- **Moving hazards** (a cyclist at 5 m/s, an e-scooter at 6 to 7 m/s, a car) need **10 to 20 m+** range to give 2 s of closing time. At VGA resolution, a small COCO model can likely find vehicles at that range, but estimating distance or time-to-collision from a single camera needs **tracking across frames** (box growth rate), not single-frame detection.
- **Latency budget for the ESP32-CAM pipeline:** MJPEG over Wi-Fi, plus decode, plus inference, plus cue, should stay at or below about 300 ms end to end. Wi-Fi jitter and a stalled stream must fail loudly (a "no camera" cue) rather than silently. That matches the prototype's existing stalled-webcam handling.
- **Cue design:**
  - Use a few distinct patterns: left, centre or right, plus urgency. GuideTouch shows recognition drops when there are more patterns.
  - Code distance as repetition rate.
  - Keep speech for rare, high-value events.
  - Use bone conduction or haptics so environmental sounds aren't masked; Biped uses bone conduction.
- **False-alert tolerance:** no number was found. As a proposal, aim for well under one false alert per minute of walking, and gate cues on path-corridor position plus approach (TTC) to suppress static off-path objects.
- **Camera placement:** a chest camera has a blind spot right at the feet and a limited upward view. It needs to be tilted or have a wide FOV to catch both head-level protrusions and near drop-offs. The OV2640's roughly 60 to 70° FOV is narrow compared with Biped's 170°.

### Gaps
- No user study giving preferred warning distance or lead time in seconds was found.
- No published acceptable false-positive rate was found.
- The OV2640 FOV is from general knowledge, not a cited source. Verify it against the specific lens fitted.

## Q4. What existing products and research systems detect, and how

### Takeaway
Commercial systems have moved from ultrasonic range sensing (Sunu, WeWALK, BuzzClip) to **wide-FOV 3D or stereo cameras with trajectory prediction** (Biped NOA), **pedestrian semantic segmentation** (.lumen) and **robotic guides** (Glidance Glide). Narrower apps handle single high-value tasks: **pedestrian signals** (OKO) and **doors, people and furniture with LiDAR** (Apple). Independent evaluations are scarce; most evidence comes from the companies.

### Cited Findings
- **Biped NOA:**
  - Chest or shoulder harness with 3D cameras covering a 170° FOV.
  - Predicts obstacle trajectories a few seconds ahead.
  - Spatial audio through bone-conduction headphones.
  - The company says it iterated with trialists.
  - No independent review was found. AppleVis users are sceptical about form factor and cane replacement.
  
  — [Ophthalmology Times 2022](https://www.ophthalmologytimes.com/view/smart-harness-alerts-visually-impaired-people-to-obstacles-while-walking); [AppleVis](https://applevis.com/comment/172263); [TSBVI 2025 presentation](https://library.tsbvi.edu/assoc_files/EEG80aGG.txt)
- **.lumen glasses (dotLumen):**
  - A headset with forehead haptics, marketed as "pedestrian autonomous driving".
  - Uses a proprietary pedestrian semantic segmentation model, said to run 100 times per second, to tell footpath from road and to find crosswalks.
  - Company claims: about 70% of a self-driving car's sensing at one-tenth the hardware size (CES 2026); CE-certified; 300 to 400+ blind testers (numbers vary by source); CES 2026 Innovation Award.
  - A blindfolded reporter navigated an indoor maze without collisions.
  
  — [New Atlas](https://newatlas.com/wearables/dotlumen-ai-glasses-blind-independence); [The Gadgeteer](https://the-gadgeteer.com/2025/12/26/what-happens-when-blind-navigation-stops-being-reactive/); [FMAI Hub CEO interview](https://fmai-hub.com/empowering-the-blind-with-pedestrian-autonomous-driving-dotlumen-ceo-interview); [CORDIS](https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/190143610/reporting)
- **Glidance Glide:**
  - A wheeled, handle-held guide with cameras, sensors and AI that steers around obstacles and brakes.
  - Recognises crosswalks, lines the user up and stops before the curb. The user decides when to go, and Glide holds a straight line to the opposite curb cut.
  - July 2026: the company reports first pilot users walking solo with Glide as their primary aid.
  
  — [Glidance product](https://www.glidance.io/product); [Glidance FAQ](https://www.glidance.io/frequently-asked-questions); [The Robot Report](https://www.therobotreport.com/glide-works-with-people-with-blindness-navigate-world-says-glidance-ceo/)
- **OKO (AEYES):**
  - iPhone app that detects and reads pedestrian signals (walk, don't walk, countdown) using the rear camera.
  - Gives distinct audio and haptic beat patterns.
  - Works offline and warns about veering during a crossing.
  
  — [Ophthalmic Edge](https://ophthalmicedge.org/patient/oko-app-makes-walk-signals-accessible); [Forbes 2023](https://www.forbes.com/sites/gusalexiou/2023/08/10/oko-app-deploys-ai-to-make-crossing-the-street-safer-for-blind-pedestrians/); [Sight Tech Global](https://sighttechglobal.com/session/why-did-the-ai-cross-the-street-the-oko-app-story/)
- **Apple Magnifier Detection Mode:**
  - Detects doors (attributes, signs, how to open), people (with distance) and furniture.
  - Requires the LiDAR Scanner, per the 2022 announcement and the current accessibility page.
  - Apple says it is not for navigation or high-risk use.
  
  — [Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/detect-doors-around-you-iph35c335575/ios); [Apple Newsroom 2022](https://www.apple.com/au/newsroom/2022/05/apple-previews-innovative-accessibility-features/); [Apple Accessibility](https://apple.com/accessibility/vision)
- **WeWALK:** ultrasonic sensor on the cane for waist-to-head obstacles. Does not detect drops. — [WeWALK support](https://support.wewalk.io/en/article/are-there-any-obstacles-objects-that-wewalk-cannot-detect-3/)
- **Sunu Band:** ultrasonic wrist band with haptic proximity, range about 2 to 4.5 m. — [New Atlas](https://newatlas.com/sunu-band-blind/50770)
- **Research systems:**
  - GuideTouch: ESP32 plus ToF plus four haptic motors.
  - Nature Comms 2025: cross-modal learning wearable with a 320 ms latency target.
  - A 3D ToF camera plus Braille display system tested with 6 legally blind users.
  
  — [arXiv 2601.13813](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.13813v2); [Nature Comms](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-58085-x)
- **VIABench (arXiv 2607.14660):** a video benchmark collected from blind individuals for visual-impairment assistance. Possibly useful for evaluating models on real egocentric footage; not reviewed in depth. — [arXiv 2607.14660](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.14660)

### Inferences
- The leading products all combine **depth or 3D sensing, or segmentation,** with object classes. None relies on 2D box detection alone. For a monocular ESP32-CAM, the closest substitutes are monocular depth models and walkable-surface segmentation.
- Single-purpose features such as signal reading (OKO) and crosswalk alignment (Glide) are high-value, well-scoped tasks that suit a phone-side model.

### Gaps
- No technical write-ups were found naming the specific network architectures used by Biped, .lumen or Glidance.
- Seeing AI, Envision and Be My AI were not researched in this pass. They are mainly scene description and OCR, not real-time obstacle avoidance.
- No head-to-head independent evaluation of the commercial devices was found.

## Q5. Which tasks need vision beyond bounding boxes?

### Takeaway
Evidence from products and user surveys points to four tasks where boxes are not enough:
- **walkable-path or sidewalk segmentation** (.lumen, Glide)
- **drop-off and curb detection**, which needs depth or segmentation
- **pedestrian signal and crosswalk recognition** (OKO, Glide)
- **head-level protrusion detection**, which needs a depth or geometry estimate rather than a class label, since branches and signs are open-ended categories

### Cited Findings
- .lumen uses pedestrian semantic segmentation to tell footpaths from roads and find crosswalks. — [New Atlas](https://newatlas.com/wearables/dotlumen-ai-glasses-blind-independence); [FMAI Hub](https://fmai-hub.com/empowering-the-blind-with-pedestrian-autonomous-driving-dotlumen-ceo-interview)
- Glide recognises crosswalks and curbs and keeps a straight line to the opposite curb cut. — [Glidance product](https://www.glidance.io/product)
- OKO reads walk and countdown signal states and detects veering. — [Ophthalmic Edge](https://ophthalmicedge.org/patient/oko-app-makes-walk-signals-accessible)
- Users name drop-offs (stairs, manholes) and thin objects (poles, bollards) as gaps the cane leaves. These are poorly served by COCO classes. — [arXiv 2601.13813](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.13813v2)
- 44% of RNIB respondents cite a lack of accessible crossings, and 71% cite uneven pavements. — [LocalGov 2025](https://www.localgov.co.uk/Street-hazards-endangering-blind-pedestrians-charity-warns/62969)
- Apple Door Detection also reads signs on doors (OCR). — [Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/detect-doors-around-you-iph35c335575/ios)

### Inferences
- **Suggested criteria for ranking models for Strider:**
  - (a) Can it flag **any** obstacle in the walking corridor at head or chest height within about 3 to 4 m? Class-agnostic depth or "obstacle vs free space" beats a fixed COCO list here.
  - (b) Can it segment walkable sidewalk vs road vs drop-off?
  - (c) Can it detect and track approaching vehicles, cyclists and e-scooters with time-to-collision?
  - (d) Does it run within a 300 ms end-to-end budget on the phone or laptop?
  - (e) Is its false-alert rate on real egocentric outdoor footage low?
- Text and sign reading and pedestrian-signal reading are better as **on-demand** modes than continuous alerts, consistent with users' dislike of verbose output.

### Gaps
- No user-study evidence ranking segmentation-based against detection-based guidance for blind walkers was found.
- No published accuracy figures for monocular drop-off or curb detection on chest-mounted, low-resolution cameras were found.
