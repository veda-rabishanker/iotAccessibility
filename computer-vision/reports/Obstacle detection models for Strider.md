# Pair depth with a licensed detector

The best vision setup for Strider is a combination, not a single detector. Rank one is **an Apache-2.0 depth model (Depth Anything V2 Small) watching the walking corridor, plus an Apache-2.0 detector fine-tuned on Strider's own chest-cam footage (RF-DETR Nano, with D-FINE-N/S as the fallback), plus frame-to-frame tracking for time-to-collision**. Rank two adds a walkable-surface segmenter running at a lower rate. The reason is that the hazards blind pedestrians most need help with are head- and chest-height protrusions, quiet fast vehicles, cyclists and e-scooters, and drop-offs. A COCO detector limited to person, vehicle, bike, dog and bag covers only the second of those. Depth covers the other two without needing a class label. Among single detectors, **YOLO26n has the best measured phone speed (about 4 ms on an iPhone 17 Pro Neural Engine, vendor figure)**, but it shares the AGPL-3.0 licence of the current YOLO11n prototype. So it only works commercially with a paid Ultralytics Enterprise licence. Open-vocabulary models (YOLOE, Grounding DINO) are most useful for auto-labelling training data, and small vision-language models belong behind an on-demand "what's around me?" button, never in the alert path. Almost every speed and accuracy figure below comes from the model's own authors. No phone benchmark exists for the DETR-family detectors, there are no published Android numbers for the depth models, and no study covers any of these models on chest-height sidewalk footage. A short on-device benchmark and a real-footage evaluation should come before any final choice.

## What blind walkers need first: corridor hazards the cane cannot reach

The ranking has to start from what goes wrong for cane and guide-dog users, not from COCO accuracy. A long cane detects ground-level obstacles within about 1 m. It cannot detect anything that is not standing on the ground or that is farther away ([GuideTouch, arXiv 2601.13813](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.13813v2)). In GuideTouch's interviews with 28 visually impaired people, respondents named three hazard types the cane misses: **head-level obstacles** (road signs, gates, horizontal bars), **drop-offs** (open manholes, stairs), and **thin objects** (poles, bollards, fencing). They also called replacing the cane "highly undesirable" ([arXiv 2601.13813](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.13813v2)). Head-level accidents are common. Secondary readings of Manduchi and Kurniawan's survey of more than 300 blind people put them at roughly 40% of respondents at least once a year, though the primary text could not be checked ([Hersh 2022, Glasgow eprint](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/271318/1/271318.pdf)). On UK streets, RNIB's 2025 poll of about 1,200 people found **82% cite pavement-parked vehicles, 71% cracked or uneven pavements, 56% temporary clutter such as bins and A-boards, and 52% sharing space with cyclists or e-scooters** ([LocalGov 2025](https://www.localgov.co.uk/Street-hazards-endangering-blind-pedestrians-charity-warns/62969)). Guide Dogs UK reports that 77% of blind or partially sighted respondents feel unsafe because of how e-scooters are ridden ([Guide Dogs](https://www.guidedogs.org.uk/how-you-can-help/campaigning/our-current-campaigns/transport/)). Quiet vehicles add to this: in a Penn State test-track study, the chance of detecting a quiet vehicle sometimes never reached 100%, even at close range ([Penn State](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/blind-pedestrians-and-quieter-vehicles-how-adding-artificial-soun-2/)).

Weighing severity, frequency and what the cane leaves uncovered gives a priority order for Strider. **First, head- and chest-height protrusions. Second, approaching vehicles, cyclists and e-scooters. Third, drop-offs. Fourth, path-blocking parked cars and scooters, where the value is earlier rerouting rather than avoiding a collision. Fifth, thin poles and temporary clutter. Sixth, uneven surfaces**, which a VGA chest camera is unlikely to resolve. The current prototype's class list covers item two well and part of item four. COCO has no classes for poles, bollards, branches, barriers, mirrors, curbs, stairs or holes. Even Open Images V7, with 600 classes, lacks traffic cone, scooter, pole and bollard ([Open Images V7 class list](https://storage.googleapis.com/openimages/v7/oidv7-class-descriptions-boxable.csv)). So the top-priority hazards are either open-ended categories (anything at head height) or ground geometry (an edge where the pavement ends), and neither is a fixed object class. This is the central reason the ranking favours **depth or geometry signals over a longer class list**. The products that lead the field point the same way. Biped uses wide-field 3D cameras with trajectory prediction ([Ophthalmology Times](https://www.ophthalmologytimes.com/view/smart-harness-alerts-visually-impaired-people-to-obstacles-while-walking)). dotLumen uses pedestrian semantic segmentation to tell footpath from road ([New Atlas](https://newatlas.com/wearables/dotlumen-ai-glasses-blind-independence)). Glidance recognises crosswalks and curbs ([Glidance](https://www.glidance.io/product)). None of these products relies on 2D boxes alone, though most of the evidence comes from the companies themselves.

The second constraint is false alarms. In a 2025 study of 13 blind and low-vision participants, unnecessary alerts added cognitive cost and slowed users without preventing collisions. Participants also reported alerts that came too late or never fired ([medRxiv 2025](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.07.07.25330998v1.full)). Hersh's review likewise says alerts should be limited to obstacles the user could actually collide with ([PMC9324285](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9324285/)). For model choice, this means **precision on "will I hit this?" matters more than recall across all classes**. Every detection should pass through a corridor-and-approach gate before it becomes a cue. A model that labels every person on a busy street is a liability. Timing completes the picture. One wearable study set an end-to-end latency target of **at most 320 ms** based on human reaction time ([Nature Communications 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-58085-x)). At a walking pace of 1.2–1.4 m/s, giving 2–3 s of warning for a static obstacle means detecting it reliably at **about 3–4 m**. A cyclist or e-scooter approaching at 5–7 m/s needs 10–20 m of range, and time-to-collision has to come from tracking box growth across frames rather than from a single frame. These distances follow from the cited latency and walking speed; no study measured them directly.

## Ranked stacks: geometry first, class labels second

The table ranks complete pipelines by how well they cover the hazard priorities above within a phone-side budget, under a licence a closed-source commercial product can ship.

| Rank | Stack | Hazards covered | Main misses | Phone latency evidence | Commercial licence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Depth Anything V2 Small (corridor depth, drop-off edges, approach) + fine-tuned RF-DETR Nano or D-FINE-N/S + tracking | Head-height protrusions (as depth "something in corridor"), vehicles, cyclists, e-scooters with time-to-collision, drop-offs (partial), named clutter | Thin poles and branches smoothed by depth; surface texture; glass | Depth: 31–34 ms on iPhone Neural Engine (Apple-reported). Detector: none published | Apache-2.0 throughout |
| 2 | Rank 1 + walkable-surface segmenter (DeepLabV3+-MobileNetV3) at 2–4 Hz | Adds sidewalk-versus-road and veering into the road; strengthens drop-off cues | Same as rank 1; road-as-sidewalk errors about 8% | Segmenter: 7.4 FPS at 512×384 on Android (authors) | Model code permissive; check dataset licence |
| 3 | YOLO26n (fine-tuned) + Depth Anything V2 Small + tracking | Same as rank 1 | Same as rank 1 | Detector: ~3.7 ms iPhone 17 Pro Neural Engine, 38.9 ms CPU ONNX (vendor) | Requires paid Ultralytics Enterprise licence |
| 4 | YOLOE-S with Strider's hazard vocabulary baked in (no depth) | Named poles, bollards, cones, barriers, e-scooters at zero-shot quality | Drop-offs, curbs, unnamed head-height objects; unreliable rare-class recall | 64–73 FPS on iPhone 12 CoreML (authors) | AGPL-3.0 |
| 5 | Current YOLO11n COCO subset (no depth) | Vehicles, cyclists, people, dogs | Head-height, drop-offs, thin objects, clutter | 56.1 ms CPU ONNX (vendor); ~40 ms on the team laptop | AGPL-3.0 |

**Rank 1 wins because depth answers the questions class labels cannot.** Depth Anything V2 Small is 24.8M parameters under Apache-2.0. Its Base, Large and Giant siblings are CC-BY-NC ([Depth Anything V2 GitHub](https://github.com/DepthAnything/Depth-Anything-V2)). Apple's CoreML conversion runs in **about 31 ms on an iPhone 12 Pro Max and 34 ms on an iPhone 15 Pro Max**, mostly on the Neural Engine ([Apple model card](https://huggingface.co/apple/coreml-depth-anything-v2-small)). That is the only phone-measured depth figure in the research, and Apple published it for its own conversion. A relative depth map supports three cues without any object class. A depth discontinuity in the lower-centre ground band flags a drop-off or stair-down. A near-depth blob in the upper part of the corridor flags a head-height protrusion. An object whose depth keeps decreasing across frames is approaching. These are design inferences: no paper reports drop-off or time-to-collision accuracy from monocular depth on a chest-mounted camera. Metric distance ("2 metres ahead") needs a scale. The cheapest source is the known chest height and tilt of the camera combined with a ground-plane fit. DA3METRIC-LARGE is Apache-licensed and outputs metric depth, but at 0.35B parameters it is probably too heavy for 8 Hz on a phone ([Depth Anything 3 GitHub](https://github.com/ByteDance-Seed/Depth-Anything-3)). The detector's role in this stack is narrower than in the prototype. It names the movers (car, cyclist, e-scooter, person, dog) so tracking can estimate time-to-collision and the cue can say what is coming, and it names clutter the user benefits from hearing about. Depth tells Strider whether something is in the way; the detector tells it what. Both feed a corridor gate that suppresses everything off-path or receding, which is what keeps false alarms down.

**Rank 2 is the eventual target, not the first build**, because its evidence is real but marginal on timing. SENSATION-DS is the closest public match to Strider's viewpoint: 2,752 chest-height pedestrian-view images with a nine-class navigation taxonomy. The fastest model, DeepLabV3+-MobileNetV3, ran at **7.383 FPS on Android at 512×384** and had the lowest rate of labelling road as sidewalk, **0.079** ([arXiv 2607.21137](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21137)). UPerNet-MobileNetV3 had the best mIoU, 0.715, but was slower. Running the segmenter at 2–4 Hz at reduced resolution to keep a "safe corridor" mask, while depth and detection run faster, fits the budget on paper. An 8% road-as-sidewalk error rate is too high for the segmenter to act alone, and the authors say benefit to blind users is not yet validated. Segmentation adds the one thing depth cannot provide: whether the flat surface ahead is footpath or road. RNIB's finding that 92% of respondents step into the road to get around obstructions ([RNIB In My Way 2025](https://media.rnib.org.uk/documents/In_My_Way_-_Navigating_pedestrian_journeys_with_sight_loss_2025_PDF.pdf)) suggests that cue is valuable. That figure comes from a search summary, not the PDF text.

**Rank 3 has the same design as rank 1 but trades licence cost for deployment certainty.** YOLO26n has the only published iPhone detector timing. It is NMS-free, which simplifies the prototype's custom parser, and Ultralytics' export tooling is the most mature available ([YOLO26 docs](https://docs.ultralytics.com/models/yolo26/); [CoreML integration](https://docs.ultralytics.com/integrations/coreml)). If an Enterprise quote is affordable, rank 3 is the lowest-engineering-risk route to a shipped product, and it could reasonably move above rank 1. **Rank 4 and rank 5 are bench tools, not product architectures.** Without depth, neither sees a drop-off or an unnamed overhead obstacle, and both carry AGPL-3.0.

## Detector shortlist: licence and unmeasured phone speed decide

The detector slot is where the user's existing plan meets a licensing wall. Ultralytics states that all YOLO-trained models, **including fine-tuned weights**, fall under AGPL-3.0 by default. It lists closed-source software and embedded edge deployments as cases that need an Enterprise licence, and it publishes no price ([Ultralytics License](https://www.ultralytics.com/license)). That covers the current YOLO11n, YOLO26, YOLOE and any Ultralytics Objects365 checkpoint. Exporting to ONNX or TFLite does not remove the obligation under Ultralytics' stated position. The detectors below are ranked for a commercial phone product.

| Rank | Detector | COCO AP | Params | Published speed | Licence | Why this rank |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | RF-DETR Nano (fine-tuned) | 48.4 | 30.5M | 2.3 ms T4 TensorRT only | Apache-2.0 | Highest small-model accuracy and best fine-tuning transfer; 384 px native input suits VGA; phone speed unknown |
| 2 | D-FINE-N / S (COCO weights) | 42.8 / 48.5 | 4M / 10M | 2.12 / 3.49 ms T4 only | Apache-2.0; Objects365 weights flagged | Small enough for phones; clean licence on COCO-only weights |
| 3 | YOLO26n (with Enterprise licence) | 40.9 | 2.4M | ~3.7 ms iPhone 17 Pro Neural Engine; 38.9 ms CPU | AGPL-3.0 or paid | Only measured phone path; best tooling; licence cost |
| 4 | MediaPipe EfficientDet-Lite0 | not published | — | 29.3 ms Pixel 6 int8 CPU | Not verified here | Measured Android number; weak, closed vocabulary |
| — | YOLO11n (current) | 39.5 | 2.6M | 56.1 ms CPU | AGPL-3.0 | Strictly worse than YOLO26n under the same licence |
| — | DEIMv2, ECDet | 43.0 (N) / 51.7 (S) | 3.6M / <10M | T4 only | Non-commercial / unverified | Excluded for a commercial product |

**RF-DETR Nano takes first place on accuracy and fine-tuning evidence, with a condition attached.** It scores 48.4 COCO AP and 57.7 on the RF100-VL domain-transfer benchmark, under Apache-2.0 for the Nano through Large sizes. The tiny Atto, Femto and Pico sizes and the XL sizes are under Roboflow's PML licence, which allows self-hosted commercial use only with a paid add-on ([rf-detr GitHub](https://github.com/roboflow/rf-detr)). The paper presents it as a detector built to be fine-tuned and claims 5.3 AP over D-FINE nano at similar latency ([arXiv 2511.09554](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.09554)). The 7.5 AP gap over YOLO26n matters for distant cyclists and small clutter. The condition is phone speed. RF-DETR Nano has about 12 times YOLO26n's parameters. Roboflow itself says its DINOv2 backbone "cannot be compressed to that same degree" as YOLO and positions it for GPU edge devices ([Roboflow](https://roboflow.com/blog/rf-detr-vs-yolo)). The repo lists no CoreML or TFLite export. If a one-day ONNX Runtime Mobile test on a mid-range Android phone shows it cannot share a 125 ms frame with depth, it drops below D-FINE.

**D-FINE-N/S is the licence-safe fallback that will more likely fit a phone.** At 4M and 10M parameters it is close to YOLO in size, and it is Apache-2.0 ([D-FINE GitHub](https://github.com/Peterande/D-FINE)). The catch is its Objects365-pretrained weights. These have better street-furniture vocabulary, but the README warns they may fall under Objects365's dataset terms and are not commercially cleared. Objects365's own site states no licence at all ([objects365.org](https://www.objects365.org/overview.html)). A Keras Hub model card nonetheless labels the Objects365 weights Apache-2.0 ([Keras HF card](https://huggingface.co/keras/dfine_medium_obj365/blob/main/README.md)). Strider should start from COCO-only weights until a lawyer has reviewed that question. DETR-style decoders with deformable attention are also a general concern for mobile neural accelerators; this is unverified, but it is the reason the plan recommends ONNX Runtime with CoreML or NNAPI execution providers rather than a fragile TFLite conversion.

**YOLO26n ranks third only because of cost.** On the engineering evidence alone it would be first. **DEIMv2 is excluded outright**: its licence says "Commercial Use of the DEIMv2 Materials is not permitted," weights included ([DEIMv2 LICENSE](https://github.com/Intellindust-AI-Lab/DEIMv2/blob/main/LICENSE.md)). That is a trap, because its Pico and N sizes look attractive on paper. One more caveat applies to every row. None of these detectors has a published robustness test for motion blur, low light, JPEG artifacts or thin-object recall. Those are exactly the conditions an OV2640 on a walking chest produces, and augmentation during fine-tuning will likely matter more than the architecture choice.

## Depth, segmentation, open vocabulary and VLMs each have a narrow job

**Depth: Depth Anything V2 Small first, Depth Anything 3 Small second.** DA3-Small is Apache-2.0, and community CoreML and LiteRT conversions exist ([CoreML conversion](https://huggingface.co/mlboydaisuke/Depth-Anything-3-Small-CoreML); [LiteRT conversion](https://huggingface.co/litert-community/Depth-Anything-3-Small)). But nobody has published a phone latency for it, and sources disagree on its size (0.08B in the repo, 24.7M on Qualcomm AI Hub). Qualcomm lists both depth models without Android timings and marks them unsupported on mobile chipsets ([Qualcomm AI Hub](https://aihub.qualcomm.com/models/depth_anything_v2)), so Android depth speed is the largest unknown in the whole stack. Monocular depth will likely fail on glass and reflections, and in low light. It may also blend thin poles and branches into the background, which is the same class of hazard detectors also miss. These failure modes come from general experience with such models, not from measurements on Strider footage. Thin vertical obstacles therefore remain the weakest-covered hazard in every ranked stack. They need explicit training labels for the detector and testing on real ESP32-CAM frames.

**Open-vocabulary detection: use it as an auto-labelling teacher, not as the deployed model.** YOLOE can bake a text vocabulary ("pole", "bollard", "construction barrier", "e-scooter") into an ordinary YOLO-shaped head with zero deployment overhead. The authors report **64–73 FPS on an iPhone 12** for the S models ([YOLOE paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.07465v2)). A YOLO26-based YOLOE-26 also exists ([PyImageSearch](https://pyimagesearch.com/2026/08/24/yolo26-open-vocabulary-object-detection-with-yoloe-26/)). But zero-shot LVIS AP of about 28 means recall on rare classes such as "bollard" will be inconsistent, and the licence is AGPL-3.0 ([THU-MIG/yoloe](https://github.com/THU-MIG/yoloe)). That makes YOLOE an excellent laptop bench tool for testing which hazard words are worth training. Grounding DINO is Apache-2.0 ([Roboflow](https://playground.roboflow.com/models/compare/grounding-dino-vs-yoloe)) and is the safer commercial pre-labeller for sampled frames. Grounding DINO 1.5 Edge reaches only about 10 FPS on a Jetson Orin NX and needs an API token, which rules it out for a phone ([arXiv 2405.10300](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2405.10300)).

**VLMs: an on-demand description mode only.** Benchmarks for walking assistance consistently find small VLMs too slow and too verbose, and weak at assessing risk. mmWalk reports that open models "struggle with risk assessment and navigational tasks" ([arXiv 2510.11520](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.11520v1)). A hazard-tuned 7B model takes **1.6–2.7 s** end to end on a desktop GPU ([Friendli](https://friendli.ai/models/xcyuan/HAG-Qwen2.5-VL-7B-RU)). Apple's FastVLM 0.5B is the most credible iPhone candidate but is described only as "near real-time", with no millisecond figure ([Apple ML Research](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/fast-vision-language-models)). A wrong "path is clear" from a VLM is a safety failure. Pedestrian-signal reading fits better as an on-demand mode too: LytNet is MIT-licensed, reports 0.93–0.99 precision on light states, and V1 ran at 21 fps on an iPhone 7 ([ImVisible](https://github.com/samuelyu2002/ImVisible)). OKO's commercial success with signal reading shows that users value this narrow task ([Ophthalmic Edge](https://ophthalmicedge.org/patient/oko-app-makes-walk-signals-accessible)).

## Strider's own footage matters more than the architecture

No permissively licensed, ready-made sidewalk-hazard checkpoint exists. Academic models such as TFP-YOLO report 93.9% mAP@0.5. But that score comes from a single Beijing dataset, the model is built on AGPL YOLOv8, and its licence is unclear ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12473299/)). It is useful as an architecture hint (an extra P2 small-object head) but not as a deployable model. The largest sidewalk-specific dataset, SideGuide, has 350K images, but its terms limit it to non-commercial research and forbid distributing derived data ([SideGuide terms](https://docs.google.com/forms/d/e/1FAIpQLScBmoVoj0d-omBOVCHGjhRislXP0TYzRqaUJOmJcqN6ylQcxQ/viewform)). Mapillary Vistas has historically required a paid licence for commercial products ([TechCrunch 2017](https://techcrunch.com/2017/05/03/mapillary-open-sources-25k-street-level-images-to-train-automotive-ai-systems)). The permissive seeds are Open Images V7 (CC BY annotations, CC BY 2.0 images) ([Open Images](https://storage.googleapis.com/openimages/web/factsfigures_v7.html)), ImVisible for pedestrian lights, and a CC BY 4.0 Roboflow relabel of the Chinese WOTR dataset whose provenance still needs checking ([Roboflow Universe](https://universe.roboflow.com/objectdetection-rfyr2/wotr_v3_phase2)). The classes that matter most to blind users (bollard, e-scooter, open car door, low branch) appear in none of the permissive sets as detection labels. They have to come from Strider's own ESP32-CAM recordings.

The practical recipe follows from that. Record walks at chest height across different routes, lighting and weather. Pre-label sampled frames with Grounding DINO, and have people review the labels. Fine-tune the chosen Apache detector with augmentation for blur, JPEG quality and low light. Each round, mine the frames where the student and the teacher disagree, and retrain. The GuideTWSI result is a warning about coverage: a model trained only on bar-type tactile paving missed dome-type paving and triggered false stops ([arXiv 2603.07060](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.07060)). Each hazard class needs its visual variants represented. Evaluation should measure what users experience, not COCO mAP: **per-class recall for hazards within about 3 m of the walking corridor, false alarms per minute of walking, and how far ahead each hazard is first detected**. No published acceptable false-alarm rate exists, so Strider will have to set its own threshold with users. VIABench, a video benchmark recorded by blind individuals, is a candidate external test set ([arXiv 2607.14660](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.14660)). One hardware caveat limits every model. A chest camera has a blind spot at the feet and limited upward view, and the OV2640's field of view (roughly 60–70°, unverified for the fitted lens) is far narrower than Biped's 170°. Camera tilt and lens choice may affect head-level and drop-off recall more than any model swap.

## Conclusion

The question "which detector is best?" turns out to be partly the wrong question. Strider's most valuable job is warning about what the cane cannot reach: objects at head height and the ground falling away. Those are geometric facts, and a monocular depth model sees them without needing the object's name. The detector's job narrows to naming and tracking the movers and the common clutter. That changes what to optimise. A smaller, licence-clean detector with a reliable phone path beats a higher-AP one that cannot share the frame budget with depth. The corridor gate that decides which detections become cues matters as much as either model, because false alarms are what make users abandon these devices.

Two decisions should come before more model work. First, choose between budgeting for an Ultralytics Enterprise licence and moving to the Apache-2.0 stack now, since staying on YOLO11n builds the product on AGPL. Second, run a one-day on-device benchmark of Depth Anything V2 Small plus RF-DETR Nano and D-FINE-N on one mid-range Android phone and one recent iPhone, using recorded ESP32-CAM frames. Android depth latency is the largest unmeasured number in the stack. If it fails the budget, the fallback is to run depth at a lower rate between detector frames, not to give up on depth, because a detector alone covers only one of Strider's three most important hazard types.
